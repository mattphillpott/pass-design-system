Functional Skills Maths Level 2
Conversion strategy

Pass Functional Skills · product page redesign · prepared 25 August 2026 · companion to PFS Product Page Redesign.html (four interactive concepts, desktop and mobile)

Executive summary

The current page asks a buyer to make four sequential decisions — invigilation style, exam date, exam-or-course, bundle tier — before it will name a price, and the price it advertises above the fold (from £157.60) is not reachable through the path it then presents (dated August slots cost £240–£272, and the recommended bundle adds a further £284). Two of those four decisions do not need to be made on this page at all: invigilation type is already constrained by the awarding body in the platform, and "book now, choose date later" is an existing product type. Removing them, and making the advertised price survive to the basket, is the single highest-value change available. My recommendation is to ship Concept 1 (Straight answer) first as the structural fix, and test Concept 3 (Deadline-led) against it as the second wave, because the deadline is the one variable that reliably predicts which package this audience should buy.

Ship first: Concept 1 — package-first, date deferred, one price that survives to checkout.

Why: it removes the two decisions the platform does not require, resolves the price contradiction, and reduces the interactive elements above the fold from 47 to 9. It is also the cheapest of the four to build, because it needs no new product types.

Test second: Concept 3 — deadline-first self-selection, which is the only concept that monetises Fast Track honestly.

Diagnosis — what the current page does to a buyer

1. The price contradiction

The hero says from £197.00 £157.60, 20% off, only 1 day left at this price. The buyer then chooses a date and sees £240–£272. Then chooses a course option and sees +£184 or +£284. The number that earned the click is not the number in the basket. This is the classic drip-pricing pattern the CMA now treats as a prohibited practice under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act — and separately, it is the most reliable basket-abandonment cause in e-commerce. Every concept fixes this by carrying the resolved total in the CTA label itself.

2. Four decisions, none of them framed

Steps 1–4 are titled in the platform's language, not the buyer's: Choose Invigilation Style, Choose Course Option, Choose Course Bundle Option. A first-time buyer does not know what invigilation is, and the difference between "Course Option" and "Course Bundle Option" is not recoverable from the page. Hick's Law is the governing principle: decision time rises logarithmically with the number of options, and it rises further when the options are not comprehensible. The 36-cell price calendar alone presents more choices than the whole rest of the page.

3. Two of the four decisions are not required here

From the codebase: ExamValidator restricts invigilation type per awarding body via allowed_invigilation_types, so invigilation is a consequence of the board, not a free choice — what the buyer actually cares about (how fast the result comes, whether a human walks them through setup) can be expressed as an outcome. And exam-date.php shows book-later-date / add-date-container as first-class product types with a post-purchase "Choose Exam Date" flow. The date can leave the buy box entirely.

An honesty problem to fix before any test runs. result-speed.php shows Fast Track results as a separately purchased upsell. The live page's "Results in as little as 2 working days" sits in the hero as though it were included. All four concepts state 2–6 working days as standard, 2 guaranteed with Fast Track. This costs a little hero punch and removes a refund-and-complaint driver.

4. Five urgency devices cancel each other out

A red sticker bar ("HURRY! ONLY 8 SPACES REMAINING"), a yellow duplicate of the same claim, a red countdown, "Only 1 day left at this price", and per-date price decay all fire simultaneously. You confirmed all five are factually true, which is what makes the waste avoidable: scarcity works through contrast, so five simultaneous alarms read as decoration and suppress the credibility of each. The concepts keep one line, adjacent to the CTA, stating both real facts once.

5. The same claim, five times

The 93% pass rate appears in the gallery card, the bullet list, a full-width green band, a red band inside step 3, and again in the comparison section. The Course Option Comparison section is a second copy of steps 3 and 4. Repetition without new information reads as sales pressure, and it pushes the reviews and FAQ — the content that answers the objections you flagged — below eight screens of scroll.

Personas and their conversion funnels

Distilled from the six segments you selected. Each has a different blocking objection, which is why the four concepts differ in what they lead with rather than only in how they look.

Priya, 27 — the deadline

Situation: nursing applicant with a conditional offer requiring Level 2 maths. Desktop at work, mobile in the evening. Will pay full price without hesitation.

Blocking objection: "Will the certificate exist in time, and will the trust accept it?"

Trigger: a named date. "Sit it Tue 25 Aug, certificate by 1 Sep."

Funnel: result-date confirmation → acceptance proof → buy. Price is not a step.

Dan, 34 — the second chance

Situation: failed GCSE maths twice, wants a teaching-assistant role. Self-funding. Has visited the page three times without buying.

Blocking objection: "Am I actually capable of passing this?"

Trigger: risk reversal — the free resit, framed as an inability to lose the fee — plus a learner story from someone who also failed twice.

Funnel: self-efficacy → risk reversal → instalments → buy.

Alisha, 19 — the apprentice

Situation: must pass Level 2 to complete her apprenticeship. Paying herself, on a phone, on a budget.

Blocking objection: "Can I spread this, and do I actually need the course?"

Trigger: honest cheapest option, visible instalments, and a plain answer to "do I need the course".

Funnel: cheapest viable option → instalment maths → buy. She is the persona most damaged by a £441 default.

Mark, 49 — the parent

Situation: buying for his 17-year-old after a GCSE resit failure. Desktop. Will phone before paying.

Blocking objection: "Is this a real qualification or a diploma mill?"

Trigger: Ofqual and awarding-body names, a phone number answered by a person, institutional plainness over sale banners.

Funnel: legitimacy → phone call → buy (often on a second session).

The structural insight. Priya, Dan and Mark are all under-served by a page organised around product configuration, and all three would be well served by a page organised around their situation. That is what separates Concepts 2, 3 and 4 from Concept 1 — Concept 1 fixes the mechanics; the others change what the page is about.

C1 — Straight answer

Pitch: the page the current one is trying to be. Same products, same prices, same claims — two decisions instead of four, and a price that does not change on the way to the basket.

Block structure — desktop

  1. Header 64px, white, phone number visible right. No promotional bar.
  2. Breadcrumb Home / Exams / this exam.
  3. Left column, sticky — gallery (exam-at-home photo, four thumbnails: exam pack, 93% card, setup video, certificate), then the Trustpilot strip.
  4. Right column, 640px — star line and review count; H1; one-sentence subhead carrying Ofqual + at-home + "two choices".
  5. Step 1 — Choose what you need. Three package rows, cheapest first. Each row: name, one-line blurb, one italic "for people who…" line, was-price and now-price right-aligned. Free-resit row carries a "93% of these learners pass" pill and is pre-selected.
  6. Step 2 — When do you want to sit it? Two options: Decide later (£0, pre-selected) and Pick a date now (+£82, "later dates cost less"). Footnote: free date change up to 7 days before.
  7. Order summary — package, exam pack (Included), exam date. Total today in 30px.
  8. CTA 56px, full width, label carries the price. Instalment line beneath.
  9. One urgency line — places left and offer end date, together, once.
  10. Proof grid 2×3, then awarding-body chips.

Block structure — mobile

  1. 52px header with tap-to-call. Gallery. H1. "from £157.60" with was-price.
  2. Three proof bullets, one urgency line, awarding-body chips.
  3. The four objection FAQs, collapsed — because on mobile this content is what replaces the left column.
  4. Sticky bottom bar (76px): selected package label, live total, "Choose options".
  5. Full-screen sheet: packages, date, summary, and a pinned footer CTA carrying the total. The configurator never competes with the content.

Visual and interaction guidance

Pass tokens throughout: --color-primary #0FBC0F for selection and CTA only — never for decoration, so selection state stays unambiguous. Selected rows take a 2px primary border plus a 3px 12%-alpha ring; unselected stay 1px #E5E7EB. Type: Inter, H1 34px/800/-0.025em, package name 17px/700, blurb 13.5px/400 at #4A5565. Prices are the only 800-weight numerals in the column, which is what makes them scannable. Amber #D97706 is reserved exclusively for the urgency line; red is not used at all — the current page's red bands compete with the error states the form genuinely needs. Micro-interactions: 150ms colour transition on selection, total counts to its new value over 200ms, no layout movement on select (the row does not grow, so the page never jumps under the cursor).

Accessibility

Package rows are a radiogroup with roving tabindex; Space and Enter select; arrow keys move within the group. Focus order runs title → packages → date → summary → CTA, matching visual order. Focus ring is the token 0 0 0 3px rgba(15,188,15,.25) plus a 2px border, never removed. Selection is never conveyed by colour alone — the radio fill and the border weight both change. Contrast: #101828 on white is 16.1:1; #6A7282 on white 4.9:1; white on #0FBC0F is only 2.4:1, so the bright primary never carries text. CTA labels are 18px/700 white on #0FBC0F (large text, 3:1 minimum, passes); anywhere small text must sit on brand colour — C6's price header — the surface is the dark end of the ramp only (#116A12 → #0F8610, all text ≥ 4.6:1) and white is used at full opacity, because a 0.85-alpha white on green drops below 4.5:1. Reversed chips use solid white with the brand colour as the text. The urgency line uses #D97706 on white (4.6:1) rather than white-on-amber. The success emerald splits the same way: #0E9F6E carries icons and borders (3:1 non-text threshold), --color-success-700 #047857 carries any text on a light surface — so C8's “Result by 9 Sep” and “In stock” lines run at 5.5:1 rather than 3.3:1. Tap targets: package rows 74px, date rows 62px, date-panel slots 96px, sticky-bar CTA 50px, sheet close 36px — all above the 44px minimum except the close affordance, which is backed by tapping the scrim and by Escape.

Rationale

  • Hick's Law. 4 decisions → 2; 47 interactive elements above the fold → 9. Decision latency falls, and each removed choice removes an exit.
  • Cognitive-load minimisation (Sweller). The buyer's language replaces the platform's. "When do you want to sit it?" needs no glossary; "Choose Invigilation Style" does.
  • Price transparency / anchoring. Listing cheapest-first sets the £157.60 anchor honestly before the £441.60 is seen, then the pre-selected recommendation does the mix work. The CTA label carries the resolved total, so the basket contains no new information.
  • Von Restorff. One urgency line against a quiet page is more salient than five against each other.
  • Goal-gradient (Hull). Numbered steps with two items make completion feel imminent; the current four-step form with an error above the CTA does the opposite.

Risks and validation

Risk: deferring the date could reduce urgency and slow the post-purchase date-booking that the platform gates behind learner-information completion. Validate: instrument date-selection rate post-purchase for 30 days; if deferred bookings stall, add a date-booking prompt to the order-confirmation email and the account dashboard rather than returning the calendar to the buy box. Risk: pre-selecting Premium raises the effective entry price against a "from £157.60" ad. Validate: this is Test 2 below, and it is segmented by traffic source.

C2 — Outcome-led

Pitch: stop selling an exam and start selling the outcome, with the free resit as the mechanism that removes the buyer's downside. For Dan, price is not the blocker — the fear of paying and failing again is.

What changes from C1

The H1 becomes the promise ("Pass Level 2 maths — or sit it again, free"). A three-tile stat band (93% / 5,399 sittings / 2–6 days) sits directly under it, sample size included, because an unqualified 93% reads as marketing and a qualified one reads as data. Step 1 is retitled "How much support do you want?". Beneath the packages, a state-aware guarantee panel: on Premium it says you cannot lose the fee; on the cheaper tiers it states the consequence plainly — a resit would be charged at £157.60; upgrading costs £100 and removes that risk. Two named testimonials sit between the urgency line and the awarding-body chips, in eye-line of the CTA.

FAQ

What happens if I fail?

On the top package your resit is free. On the others a resit is charged at £157.60. 93% of people who complete the course pass first time.

Rationale

  • Loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky). "You cannot lose the fee" is roughly twice as motivating as an equivalently-sized gain claim. Stating the un-guaranteed downside on the cheaper tiers is the same mechanism working on the mix.
  • Cialdini — social proof and authority. Named people in the buyer's own situation, plus the awarding bodies, placed at the decision rather than eight screens below it.
  • Self-efficacy (Bandura). Dan does not need to be told the exam is easy; he needs evidence that someone like him passed. Hence "failed GCSE twice" in the attribution line.
  • Risk of over-claiming. The guarantee must match the T&Cs exactly, including the resit window and any conditions. Legal sign-off is a gating task in week 1.

C3 — Deadline-led

Pitch: ask the one question the buyer arrives with. "When do you need your result?" has three answers, and each one determines package, date and Fast Track — so one question replaces four.

What changes

The buy box opens with three timescale options (within 2 weeks / within a month or two / no fixed deadline). Selecting one resolves a single recommended plan card with one price, an explanatory band naming the actual result date ("Sit it Tue 25 Aug, certificate by 1 Sep — the last sitting that clears before September course starts"), and a "change my timescale or build it myself" escape. Until a timescale is picked, the card area holds a quiet placeholder rather than a wall of options. This is the only concept that sells Fast Track, and it sells it where it is genuinely useful — which is also why it is the only concept with new revenue rather than only better mix.

FAQ

How fast do I get my result?

2–6 working days with remote invigilation, 6–16 with a live human invigilator. Fast Track guarantees the 2-day end if you're against a deadline.

Rationale

  • Self-selection over configuration. The buyer answers a question about themselves — which they can do — instead of about the product, which they cannot.
  • Concreteness. "Certificate by 1 Sep" is a verifiable claim; "fast track results" is a slogan. Concrete dates are also the only honest way to sell Fast Track.
  • Progressive disclosure. One question, then one answer. Working memory is never asked to hold four partial decisions.
  • Risk: the three timescales must map to genuinely available sittings, so the copy has to be generated from live Appointedd availability, not hard-coded. If a named date sells out and the page still promises it, this concept becomes the most damaging of the four. Validate with a stock-out state before launch.

A/B test plan

No baseline analytics were supplied, so every number below is derived from the stated assumptions. Replace the four inputs in the calculation block and the durations recompute directly.

Test 1 — Structural simplification (highest priority)

Hypothesis. Because the current buy box requires four decisions before revealing a price that contradicts the advertised one, replacing it with a package-first layout in which the resolved total appears in the CTA will increase add-to-basket rate for all traffic, with the largest effect on mobile.

Primary metric: add-to-basket rate (sessions reaching basket ÷ product-page sessions). Secondary: checkout completion, revenue per session, AOV, package mix, product-page exit rate, time to first interaction, phone-call rate. Guardrails: refund rate, Fast Track attach rate, post-purchase date-booking rate within 30 days.

Test 2 — Default package

Hypothesis. Because the free resit resolves the dominant objection for returners who have failed before, pre-selecting the free-resit package will increase revenue per session versus pre-selecting exam-plus-course, despite a lower add-to-basket rate.

Primary metric: revenue per session (not add-to-basket — the arms trade off against each other, so a conversion-only reading would mislead). Secondary: AOV, package mix, add-to-basket, refund rate. Three arms: Premium default / Exam+course default / nothing pre-selected. Three arms at the numbers above means ~26,000 sessions, so run this only at 5,000+ weekly sessions, or drop the third arm.

Pre-registered segment: paid-search traffic that landed on a "from £157.60" ad. If the Premium default underperforms there specifically, the answer is source-aware defaulting, not a global change.

Test 3 — Deadline-first (C3) vs the Test 1 winner

Hypothesis. Because a large share of this audience is buying against an external deadline, leading with "when do you need your result?" will increase add-to-basket and add incremental Fast Track revenue versus a package-first layout.

Primary: add-to-basket. Secondary: Fast Track attach rate, revenue per session, dated-slot share. Guardrail: complaints or refunds citing a missed result date — the metric that tells you whether the named-date promise is safe.

Post-test evaluation and next steps

Reading the result

  • Do not stop early. Fix the sample target in advance and read once. Peeking at a running test roughly triples the false-positive rate.
  • Read segments as hypotheses, not findings. Ten segments guarantee one "significant" result by chance. A segment win is the input to the next test, never a rollout decision.
  • Watch for a Simpson's-paradox split. A flat overall result hiding a mobile win and a desktop loss is the most likely shape here, given the device mix, and it points to shipping per-breakpoint rather than abandoning the concept.
  • Check novelty decay in returning visitors before believing a large week-1 effect.
  • Attribute revenue honestly. If C4 wins add-to-basket but shifts orders to discounted phone sales, blended revenue per session is the only fair judge.

If C1 wins

  1. Roll out to the other Functional Skills product pages (English, Level 1) — the same configurator powers them, so the gain should replicate.
  2. Take the fix upstream into the basket and checkout, which still carry the old option language.
  3. Then Test 3 (deadline-first), which is the next structural idea rather than a refinement.

Follow-up experiments, in order of expected value

  1. Source-aware defaults. Paid-search arrivals from a "from £157.60" ad see exam-only pre-selected; organic and direct see the free-resit package. Directly tests the anchoring conflict.
